A note on the record

This account separates official index entries, contemporary reporting, later reconstruction, and what remains unknown. Newspapers repeatedly vary the spellings of surnames and give conflicting versions of how the car entered the case. Those conflicts are shown, not smoothed away. Descriptions of the victims are kept non-graphic where detail does not advance the record.

01

The two cousins

The names that the state recorded

On Thursday, June 2, 1927, Jennie Monica and Edith Fonzo were found dead near Inkerman in Jenkins Township. Later local reporting describes the nineteen-year-olds as cousins and, citing their death certificates, says both suffered gunshot wounds to the head.

The clearest fixed points are unusually close together in Pennsylvania’s official index. Edith appears as certificate 63094; Jennie follows as certificate 63095. Both entries say Luzerne County and June 2. The numbers place the records consecutively in the index; they do not establish the sequence of events. Certificate copies reproduced in section 08 carry stamped file numbers one thousand lower, 62094 and 62095—a conflict this edition records rather than resolves.

Later local reporting places Jennie at 165 Moyallen Street and Edith at 368 North Washington Street. “Jennie Monica” and “Edith Fonzo” are the spellings in the state index. Contemporary newspapers also printed Monico, Fonza, and other variants—important when searching archives, but not grounds to overwrite the official forms.

02

The last night

A theater plan, reconstructed later

The earliest wire dispatch located for this project said only that the cousins left home early Wednesday evening. A much later review by the Times Leader supplied a fuller account: on Decoration Day, May 30, they reportedly met Samuel Marranca, 24, and John Falcone, 23, at Rocky Glen amusement park. The four were said to have arranged a trip to a Pittston theater for Wednesday, June 1.

That version is useful, but its evidentiary position matters. The publicly accessible source is a 2025 reconstruction of the 1927 investigation—not a surviving statement by either woman and not testimony tested at a murder trial. It should be read as reported investigative history.

03

The road at Inkerman

What was found—and what reporting inferred

Later local reporting locates the scene along an isolated roadway in the Old Boston Settlement of Inkerman and places the bodies roughly 75 feet apart. The Times Leader, attributing the injury details to the women’s death certificates, reported that Jennie had been shot twice in the head and was found without clothing; her clothes were found in the area. Edith was clothed, with ripped garments, a gunshot wound to the head, and a fractured skull.

A later writer interpreted Edith’s torn clothing as evidence that she fought an attacker. That is an interpretation, not a quoted forensic conclusion. Nor does Jennie’s state of undress, standing alone, establish a particular sexual offense. The available public sources do not justify turning either circumstance into a clinical finding.

Early syndicated reports added vivid details about the road, the automobile, and possible witnesses. Some differ in particulars. Copies of both death certificates are now reproduced in section 08, but they record cause of death, not the scene; the investigative file remains unobtained. This page therefore does not combine every newspaper detail into one supposedly settled scene.

Precision note

Evidence is not implication

Clothing condition can be evidence. It does not, by itself, establish motive, assault type, or a complete sequence of events. The distinction matters because no murder trial was located in the reviewed public record.

04

The Chrysler

One car, two surviving accounts

An automobile became the investigation’s central physical thread. A June 3 Associated Press dispatch linked a wrecked car belonging to Samuel Maranca’s brother to the case. It said Carmel Maranca reported the car stolen to Pittston police about an hour before the bodies were found; it did not say when the alleged theft occurred.

The Times Leader’s 2025 reconstruction gives a different account: the Chrysler was traced to Sam’s brother—named “Carmella” in that article—who reportedly told state police he had loaned it to Sam the day before. The early dispatch calls the brother “Carmel.” It is possible that reporting captured different statements at different moments, but the sources reviewed here do not reconcile them.

05

The Buffalo lead

Two suspects named; no murder trial located

Newspapers quickly centered suspicion on Samuel Marranca (also printed Maranca) and John Falcone. According to the later reconstruction, Falcone traveled to Buffalo with Carmel Marletti, 23. Buffalo authorities briefly detained Falcone and reportedly released him before Pennsylvania officers arrived with a warrant. Marranca had also disappeared.

The same reconstruction says Falcone later made an incriminating admission to a barber friend. No transcript, signed statement, sworn testimony, or court finding authenticating that reported admission was located for this project. It remains newspaper-reported hearsay—not a confession that can simply be presented as fact.

Suspicion shaped the search. It did not resolve the murders.

06

The accessory trial

An acquittal on an accessory charge

Carmel Marletti was tried not for killing either woman, but as an alleged accessory after the fact. On September 15, 1927, a Luzerne County jury acquitted him. In November, District Attorney Thomas M. Lewis withdrew a second indictment against him.

The legal boundary is easy to lose in a retelling: an accessory acquittal did not decide who committed the homicides. No source reviewed for this edition documents a murder trial of Marranca or Falcone. Calling either man a killer would therefore go beyond the available record.

07

The search after 1927

The search continued after 1927

The case did not end with the accessory trial. In May 1931, according to later reproduction of local reporting, Detective Leo Grohowski went first to Buffalo and then to the area outside San Antonio after receiving information that the wanted men were living under assumed names. That account does not report an arrest resulting from the search, and the reviewed public sources do not document a murder trial or conviction.

Later people with the same surnames—or even the same full names—cannot responsibly be identified as these suspects without matching records. The public sources reviewed through July 24, 2026, revealed no later murder trial or conviction. That statement describes the sources found; it is not a claim about the contents or present status of any nonpublic law-enforcement file. None of the later leads established who killed Jennie and Edith.

Graves & records

What the stones and the certificates show

Both women are buried at St. Mary’s Cemetery in Hanover Township. Jennie Monica’s marker carries the words at left—the historical language chosen by those who memorialized her.

The phrase should not be made to speak beyond the stone. It is memorial language, not this site’s judgment and not a forensic finding about what happened. No corresponding inscription for Edith is asserted here because none was verified in the sources reviewed.

The images below were supplied to this project as copies from the two Find a Grave memorial pages. They were not retrieved directly from a Pennsylvania archive, and no cemetery register, plot identifier, or grave coordinates have been obtained.

Three readings of one birth date

Jennie Monica’s birth date is not settled. Three surviving sources give three different answers, and this site does not choose among them.

  1. Carved marker June 2?, 1907

    The final digit is worn and lichen-spotted. It reads as an 8 or a 9 depending on the light; the photographs here do not resolve it.

  2. Find a Grave index June 29, 1907

    The memorial page transcribes the stone as June 29. That is one reader’s interpretation of the same weathered carving, not an independent record.

  3. Certificate copy July 29, 1907

    The handwritten date-of-birth field appears to read July, not June. The hand is looped and the month is not perfectly unambiguous.

One internal detail is worth recording without overstating it. The certificate also gives her age as 19 years, 10 months, which is arithmetically consistent with a birth in July 1907 and not with one in June. That consistency is suggestive, not decisive: the form was completed from information her father supplied two days after her death, and the marker was cut later. Edith Fonzo’s birth date is not in conflict because no source reviewed here supplies a full one—her certificate copy leaves the field blank and her marker gives the year alone.

What these copies do and do not settle

  • They confirm the burial. Both forms record burial at St. Mary’s, Hanover, on June 4, 1927—two days after the bodies were found. Burial is no longer supported only by later reporting and a crowdsourced page.
  • They corroborate the addresses. The informants’ addresses match the two Wilkes-Barre street numbers reported elsewhere on this page.
  • The numbers do not match the index citation. Pennsylvania’s 1927 death indices are cited on this site for certificates 63094 and 63095. These images are stamped 62094 and 62095. Both pairs are consecutive and end in the same two digits, so the pairing of the two records is not in doubt; the leading figures are. The discrepancy is recorded rather than resolved, because the index scans were not re-examined for this edition.
  • They do not identify a killer. The medical certificates state cause and manner. They name no assailant and decide nothing about who was responsible.
  • The mothers’ maiden names invite a reading, not a conclusion. Jennie’s mother is written as Rose Finno and Edith’s as Lena Fino. If those are the same surname, the two were maternal first cousins—consistent with the newspapers’ description of them as cousins. The hands differ and no birth or marriage record was obtained, so this is offered as an observation about the documents.
  • Nothing here locates the graves. No cemetery register, plot identifier, or grave coordinates have been obtained.

Documentary appendix · sources & method

A record with visible seams

Research reviewed through July 24, 2026. Links open the source itself, not a generic search page.

01

Start with the state

The Pennsylvania death indices establish spellings, county, date, and certificate numbers. They do not supply the full circumstances of death.

02

Keep reports in time

A next-day wire dispatch shows what investigators and reporters said immediately. Later articles can add archival context, but may also inherit old errors.

03

Name the gaps

Files and testimony not located or obtained, conflicting car accounts, and an unverified confession allegation are treated as limits, not invitations to speculate.

Remember the distinction

Documented does not mean solved.

Jennie Monica and Edith Fonzo were victims of homicide. The surviving public sources name suspects and trace a long search, but they do not supply a murder verdict. The honest ending is the unresolved one.
